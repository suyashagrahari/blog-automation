## Phase 2 — gap

**Table stakes** (present on all the prose pages): the festival's date, brother/sister/cousin
segmentation, a stock of example lines, some Hindi.

**The gap:** the grammar of a named wish. Nobody covers where in the sentence the name goes; the
choice between a full name, a nickname and a kinship term; why a filled template is visibly a
filled template; the fact that Devanagari has no capital letter to mark a name; or how to name two
siblings without excluding the rest.

**Stale data:** `mynameart.com` still carries "2024" in its title tag. None of the seven cites any
research at all, so there is no stale statistic to supersede — there is no statistic.

**Unanswered questions raised and dropped:** the listicles all print "Dear [Name]" style templates
and never acknowledge that the reader will send them to someone who has received twenty of the
same shape.

**Fan-out sub-queries → became H2s:**
1. what is a Raksha Bandhan wish with a name → *What a named Raksha Bandhan wish actually is*
2. where does the name go in the sentence → *Where the name goes: three positions, three tones*
3. full name or nickname or bhai → *Which name: Rohit, Chintu, or bhai*
4. why do personalised wishes look fake → *The mail-merge seam*
5. Hindi or English script for the name → *The name in Devanagari…*
6. wishing two brothers at once → *Two brothers, one message*
7. when not to use a name → *When to leave the name out*

**Angle:** wins by being the only post on this SERP that treats a named wish as a writing problem
rather than an image problem, using vocative-position evidence, Hindi kinship-term research and
Snell on Devanagari, grounded in 2,739 pages of first-party publishing behaviour.

## Phase 3 — sources (research lane: forms of address, kinship terms as address)

All five were fetched and read in this session. Three PDFs were converted with `pdftotext` in the
sandbox and the quoted claims located in the extracted text.

1. **kops.uni-konstanz.de** — González López & Schmid, *The Linguistic Review* 40(1) 77–106,
   published online 20 Dec 2022, CC BY 4.0. Acceptability-judgment task, 56 native Spanish
   speakers (mean age 28.3, range 20–57; 40 F / 16 M), 7-point Likert; initial vocatives received
   the highest mean ratings. Restates Zwicky (1974): initial = call (attention), middle/final =
   addressee (maintain contact); middle → politeness/formal speech, final → informal. **Peer-
   reviewed, open access, full text read.** *Limitation stated in the body: the data is Spanish.*
2. **journals.rudn.ru** — Suryanarayan & Khalil, *Russian Journal of Linguistics* 25(1) 125–146,
   2021. Open-ended questionnaires, 70 Hindi respondents (educated middle class, 20–85). Eight
   Hindi terms for cousins; *dada* vs *nana*; reports Yoon (2007) on the Korean-Australian brothers
   switching between first name (equality) and *hyeng* (seniority); kinship categories used to
   address a status-superior adult "to avoid the disrespect brought by using first names."
   **Peer-reviewed, open access, full text read.**
3. **ijhssi.org** — Maloth, *IJHSSI* 3(4) 7–9, April 2014. Kinship terms as terms of *reference*
   versus terms of *address* in Lambada: *devar* → addressed as *bhaya*/*bapu*/first name; *jeth* →
   *vadari*/*bapu*; *sasu* → *ma*/*mami*/*pupi*; spouses addressed by interjection or by the eldest
   child's name. **Full text read. Low-tier journal, observation- and interview-based, no sample
   size stated — flagged in `honestAssessment`.**
4. **hindiurduflagship.org** — Snell, *Transliterating Devanagari* (University of Texas at Austin).
   Rough romanisation "leaves too many ambiguities"; use the spelling the individual uses in
   English (*baccan* vs Bachchan) and "finding out the details may call for some research"; names
   that double as abstract nouns (आशा, शांति, प्रकाश); गोबर in Premchand's *Godān* as both a
   character's name and cow-dung, because Devanagari is single-case. **Scholarly reference work,
   full text read, no publication date stated → `publishedDate` omitted.**
5. **emerginginvestigators.org** — Gordon & Adams, *Journal of Emerging Investigators*,
   19 Feb 2024. Non-white students 2.55× more likely to experience racial/ethnic microaggressions;
   commonest reactions exhaustion, embarrassment, lost sense of belonging. **Abstract only was
   read** (full text is a separate PDF, not retrieved) — recorded in the audit. CC BY-NC-ND, so
   nothing is reproduced.
