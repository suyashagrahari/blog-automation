## Phase 2 — Gap

**Table stakes** (must be present or the post reads incomplete): the conventional
prosperity/light wish; wishes split by relationship; a Hindi option with transliteration;
short lines that fit a card; a sign-off list; whether a non-Hindu may send one.

**The gap — five things none of the five pages do:**

1. **Anatomy.** Nobody breaks a card into greeting / personal middle / forward-looking
   close / sign-off, and nobody says the middle is the only part that carries information.
   All five hand over pre-written lines that *replace* the middle.
2. **The four-tier Indian reality.** All five write for family, friends and "coworkers".
   None writes for a landlord, a watchman, or the person who cleans the flat — a tier
   that exists in almost every urban Indian Diwali and needs completely different wording.
3. **Which of the five days it lands on.** All five describe the five days as festival
   trivia; none says a card read on 6 November should be worded differently from one
   opened on 8 November.
4. **Length as a constraint with a number.** "Keep it short" appears; "35–45 words is what
   fits, and a second paragraph usually weakens it" does not.
5. **A bad year.** None of the five acknowledges that "may this Diwali bring you
   prosperity" can land badly after an illness, a death or a job loss.

**Stale data:** the two Hallmark pages are 2020 and 2022 and carry no dates at all beyond
the article date; every page's festival description is undated and evergreen. Nothing to
supersede statistically, because nobody cites a statistic.

**Fan-out sub-queries** → became H2s/FAQs: what do you actually write inside; how long;
in Hindi or English; for a boss; for a neighbour; for staff; when it should arrive; what
to write after a bereavement; is it fine to send if you don't celebrate.

**Angle:** wins by being the only page that treats the blank middle of the card as the
writing problem — four parts, a 35–45 word budget, four recipient tiers including a
landlord and house help, and day-specific wording for 6–10 November 2026 — grounded in
SubhSandesh's own 6.9-hour median gap between a page's first save and its last edit.

## Phase 3 — Sources

Research lane for this post: **handwriting versus typing, expressive writing, and what
makes a written message feel effortful to a reader.** Deliberately kept out of the giver/
recipient gift-psychology, homesickness, B2B gratitude, Hindi honorifics, regional-day,
workplace-recognition, dormant-tie and grief lanes held by siblings, and out of the rakhi
card post's four sources (`indiapost.gov.in`, `bmj.com`, `uploads-ssl.webflow.com`,
`cs.cmu.edu`) and its print-vs-link argument.

| Source | What it actually says (verified in the fetched text) | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino, *It Doesn't Hurt to Ask: Question-Asking Increases Liking*, JPSP 113(3), Sep 2017 — hbs.edu PDF, full text read | Study 3 coded real speed dates: "an 8% difference in follow-up question rates in this sample is associated, on average, with success on one additional date over an evening of 20 speed dates"; the authors state the relationship cannot be read causally | The case for ending the middle line with a question |
| Lyubomirsky, Sousa & Dickerhoof, *The Costs and Benefits of Writing, Talking, and Thinking About Life's Triumphs and Defeats*, JPSP 90(4), Apr 2006 — author's own PDF, full text read | Study 2 N=111 wrote/talked/thought about their happiest experience 15 min a day for 3 days; Study 3 N=112. Writing about a negative event beat thinking about it; students who **wrote about and analysed** their happiest moments showed **reduced** well-being versus those who replayed them | Why the second paragraph — the one that explains the happy memory — weakens the card |
| Pennebaker, *Expressive Writing in Psychological Science*, Perspectives on Psychological Science 13(2), 2018 — Northeastern PDF, full text read | The 1983 study: 4 days × 15 min writing about trauma halved student health-centre visits over 6 months; "the overall effect size of expressive writing on health across over 100 studies averages about .16 (Cohen's d)" | The honest limit on what a written message does — and why it helps the writer more than the reader |
| Smoker, Murphy & Rockwell, *Comparing Memory for Handwriting Versus Typing*, Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society, 2009 — UCF STARS record, **abstract only** ("This document is currently not available here") | Recall and recognition for common words was better when words were handwritten than typed; attributed to the additional context of the more complex writing task | Why writing the card by hand changes what you write, not just how it looks |
