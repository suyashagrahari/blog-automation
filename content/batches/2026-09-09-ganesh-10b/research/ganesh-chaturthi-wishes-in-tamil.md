## Phase 2 — gap analysis

**Table stakes** (all five carry these, so this post must too): the festival's Tamil name;
a block of wishes in Tamil script; the 2026 date; some mention of kozhukattai or prasadam.

**The gap.** Not one of the five treats the wish as a set of decisions the sender is
actually making:

1. **Which name.** விநாயகர் சதுர்த்தி, பிள்ளையார் சதுர்த்தி and கணேஷ் சதுர்த்தி are
   three different registers. No page says so.
2. **Which word for "obstacle".** இடையூறு (native) vs விக்கினம் (from Sanskrit *vighna*).
   Tamil has a century-old purism movement about exactly this and no wishes page mentions it.
3. **Which register.** Centamil (literary) vs koduntamil (spoken). Every wish on every one
   of those pages is centamil, silently.
4. **Which script.** Tamil script vs Romanised Tanglish. Nobody quantifies it.
5. **What the words mean.** Not one page gives Roman transliteration plus an English gloss,
   which is what a second-generation or non-Tamil-reading sender actually needs.

**Stale data.** oneindia is stamped 2025 and carries last year's date. Anything dated 2025
in this SERP is now wrong by a year.

**Unanswered questions.** What vazhthu literally means; why "Ganesh" written in Tamil needs
a letter that pure Tamil does not have; whether the same wish works for a Sri Lankan or
Malaysian Tamil reader; what the Chennai muhurat is.

**Fan-out sub-queries → H2s.** What is it called in Tamil / how to write a Tamil wish /
which Tamil word for obstacle / written vs spoken Tamil / Tamil script vs English letters /
kozhukattai vs modak / how to send it / when not to.

**Angle (`batchMeta.angle`):** wins by being the only Tamil Ganesh Chaturthi page that
treats the name, the word for "obstacle" and the register as choices the sender is making —
Vinayagar vs Pillaiyar, இடையூறு vs விக்கினம், centamil vs koduntamil — with every wish
carrying transliteration and an English gloss, against SubhSandesh's platform-wide 49.6%

### 1. Tamil Lexicon (Digital Dictionaries of South Asia, University of Chicago) — **subject test PASS**

`https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/tamil-lex_query.py?qs=%E0%AE%AA%E0%AE%BF%E0%AE%B3%E0%AF%8D%E0%AE%B3%E0%AF%88%E0%AE%AF%E0%AE%BE%E0%AE%B0%E0%AF%8D&searchhws=yes`
(University of Madras *Tamil Lexicon*, 1924–1936; DSAL digital edition publishes no date.)

Verified headwords, quoted only in fragments:

- **பிள்ளையார்** *piḷḷaiyār* (p. 2714) — "1. மகன் [son]. … 2. Gaṇēša; விநாயகர்." So the Tamil
  name for Ganesha is literally "the child".
- **பிள்ளையார்சதுர்த்தி** *piḷḷaiyār-caturtti* (p. 2714) — "A festival in honour of Gaṇēša.
  See விநாயகசதுர்த்தி." The Tamil Lexicon carries Pillaiyar Chaturthi as its own headword.
- **பிள்ளையார்சவுதி** *piḷḷaiyār-cavuti* (p. 2714) — a third name, marked `Loc.` (local).
- **பிள்ளையார்சுழி** *piḷḷaiyār-cuḻi* (p. 2714) — "எழுதத் தொடங்குங்கால் மங்கலமாக வரையப்படும்
  'உ' என்ற குறி" — the auspicious 'உ' mark drawn when beginning to write.
- **வாழ்த்து** *vāḻttu* (p. 3620) — "1. Benediction, felicitation; ஆசி. 2. Praise. **3.
  Invocation or praise of the deity at the beginning of a religious or literary work**, one of
  three maṅkaḷācaraṇai." Verb வாழ்த்து-தல் = "To felicitate, congratulate, bless."
- **கொழுக்கட்டை** *koḻu-k-kaṭṭai* (p. 1160) — "Bolus-like preparation of rice flour usually with
  coconut scrapings, sugar, etc.; வெல்லம் முதலியன சேர்த்து அரிசிமாவால் உருண்டைவடிவாகச் செய்யும்
  சிற்றுண்டி வகை."
- **மோதகம்** *mōtakam* (p. 3385) — sense 2: "A bolus-like preparation of rice-flour.
  **See கொழுக்கட்டை.**" The lexicon sends *modakam* to *kozhukattai*, not the other way round.
- **இடையூறு** *iṭai-y-ūṟu* (p. 288) — "Impediment, obstruction, hindrance; விக்கினம்."
- **விக்கினம்** *vikkiṉam* (p. 3631) — "n. **vighna**. Obstacle, impediment, hindrance;
  இடையூறு." Marked as the Sanskrit loan and glossed with the native word.
- **அறுகு** *aṟuku* (p. 179) — "Harialli grass … used in ceremonies, *Cynodon dactylon*;
  அறுகம்புல்."
- **விநாயகன்** *vināyakaṉ* (p. 3678) — "Gaṇēša, as the remover of obstacles."

**Source flaw, recorded honestly:** the lexicon's **விநாயகசதுர்த்தி** entry (p. 3678) defines
the day as "the fourth titi of the bright half of the lunar month of **Cirāvaṇam**"
(Śrāvaṇa), whereas modern panchangs place it in Bhādrapada / Āvaṇi. The post therefore cites
this dictionary **only for word meanings, never for the calendar month**, and takes every
date from Drik Panchang.

Query URLs cited in the post: the வாழ்த்து lookup and the பிள்ளையார் lookup. Two URLs, one
domain — `dsal.uchicago.edu` goes from 1 of 3 (the Kannada sibling) to 2 of 3.

### 2. Harold F. Schiffman, "Language Policy and Linguistic Culture in Tamilnadu" — **subject test PASS, scholarly**

`https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~haroldfs/sars238/tamil238.html`
Chapter 6 of *Linguistic Culture and Language Policy* (Routledge), self-archived open by the
author at the University of Pennsylvania. No publication date on the page; the book is 1996,
so `publishedDate` is **omitted** from `citation` per `structured-data.md`.

Verified, in the author's words:

- "it is only because of the **severely diglossic** nature of the language that this is
  possible. The literary dialect of Tamil (the (H) variety) exhibits few if any changes that
  have occurred since about the time of its **thirteenth century codification by the
  grammarian Pavanandi**."
- "the L-variety (**spoken Tamil, which has changed radically since the thirteenth
  century**) is usually treated as if it did not exist."
- "**Literacy is rising in Tamil every decade, but diglossia shows no signs of diminishing.**"
- "it could be argued that Tamil linguistic culture is a **multiglossic** linguistic culture,
  rather than 'merely' a diglossic one."
- Purism chronology: "**Maraimalai Adigal takes a vow to speak only pure Tamil (1915-6)**";
  the movement is "known generally in Tamil as the **tanit tamiR iyakkam**"; Caldwell's
  *Comparative Grammar* 1856 and Caminataiyar's rediscovery of the ancient texts on
  21 October 1880 are the milestones before it.
- On purism as a strategy: "movements to cleanse themselves of elements that sap their
  strength … What often begins as a language maintenance tactic then often becomes a corpus
  planning strategy."

### 3. Bharathi Raja Chakravarthi, Vigneshwaran Muralidaran, Ruba Priyadharshini, John P. McCrae, "Corpus Creation for Sentiment Analysis in Code-Mixed Tamil-English Text" — **subject test PASS, peer-reviewed AND open access**

`https://aclanthology.org/2020.sltu-1.28/` (PDF read in full via `pdftotext`).
Proceedings of the 1st Joint Workshop on SLTU and CCURL, LREC 2020, May 2020.

Verified figures:

- 184,573 Tamil YouTube comments were scraped from 2019 film-trailer videos.
- After discarding monolingual comments and applying a 5–15 word length filter,
  **15,744 Tanglish sentences** remained — 169,833 tokens, vocabulary 30,898,
  17,926 sentences, ~10 tokens per post.
- "**Most comments were written in Roman script** with either Tamil grammar with English
  lexicon or English grammar with Tamil lexicon. Some comments were written in Tamil script
  with English expressions in between."
- "Due to their pervasiveness of English online, code-mixed Tamil-English (**Tanglish**)
  sentences are **often typed in Roman script**."
- Inter-annotator agreement, Krippendorff's α = 0.6; minimum three annotators per sentence.

**Cap note:** this takes `aclanthology.org` to **3 of 3** across both batches (Marathi,
Hindi, this). No further ACL source may be added by any wave-3 sibling. The arXiv mirror

### 4. Richard Ishida, "Tamil orthography notes" — **subject test PASS**

`https://r12a.github.io/scripts/taml/ta.html`, updated **28 April 2026** (the page states
its own update date). Ishida is the former W3C Internationalisation Activity Lead; this is
the reference script-notes series used for W3C i18n layout work.

Verified:

- The Tamil script is an **abugida**. "**Tamil is diglossic: the classic form is preferred
  for writing and public speaking … the colloquial, spoken form differs widely from the
  written.**" (An independent second confirmation of source 2.)
- **12 independent vowel letters**; post-consonant vowels use **11 combining vowel signs**,
  with 3 pre-base glyphs and 3 circumgraphs.
- "Tamil uses **18 consonant letters for pure Tamil words, supplemented by 6 more Grantha
  consonant signs used for English and Sanskrit loan words.**" Character index lists the
  Grantha consonants as **ஜ, ஸ, ஶ, ஷ, ஹ**. Four further non-native sounds are written by
  preceding a consonant with **0B83 āytam**.
- Vowel absence uses a **visible** vowel killer, **0BCD puḷḷi**. Only **two** conjunct
  ligatures exist: **க்ஷ** and **ஶ்ரீ**.
- "In **1978**, in an attempt to simplify the script, the government of Tamil Nadu proposed
  the reform of certain letters and syllables. **These reforms only spread in India and the
  digital world, whereas Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia, Mauritius, Reunion** and other Tamil
  speaking regions continue to use the traditional syllables."
- Tamil is "spoken by over 65,500,000 people in India, Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia and
  Mauritius".
- "There are **no punctuation marks in the Tamil Unicode block**"; Western punctuation is
  used.

**The load-bearing derivation:** கணேஷ் ("Ganesh") requires **ஷ**, one of the Grantha
consonants the script imported for Sanskrit and English. விநாயகர் and பிள்ளையார் and
சதுர்த்தி are spellable entirely in the 18 native consonants. That is a checkable claim about
keystrokes, not an opinion, and no page in the SERP makes it.

New domain. `r12a.github.io` → 1 of 3.

## Honest assessment (carried into the JSON)

The SERP is four content farms and a Pinterest pin, none of which explains a word it prints,
and the differentiators here are checkable rather than stylistic: the Tamil Lexicon's own
cross-reference from *modakam* to *kozhukattai*, its gloss of *vikkiṉam* as a Sanskrit loan
for *iṭaiyūṟu*, the fact that கணேஷ் needs a Grantha letter that விநாயகர் does not, and a
Chennai muhurat none of them prints. Against that, subhsandesh.in has no Tamil-language
authority and no Ganesh authority — the template was live for days and has produced zero
pages — so nothing here is a prediction about rank. The clearest risk is language, not SEO:
the Tamil wishes were composed and checked headword-by-headword against the Tamil Lexicon,
but no native Tamil reviewer has seen them, and one clumsy line loses this audience faster
than any ranking factor.
