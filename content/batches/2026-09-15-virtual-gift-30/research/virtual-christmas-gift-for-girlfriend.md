## 3. Sources — 4 total, 3 topical peer-reviewed open access, 1 context statistic

All four were fetched and read in this session. Domain caps checked against all

### S1 — Ogden et al. (2024), *PLoS ONE* 19(7) — **full text read**

`https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/23369/`
*Distortions to the passage of time for annual events: exploring why Christmas
and Ramadan feel like they come around more quickly each year.* Published
2024-07-10, CC BY.

Verified from the deposited PDF (17 pp.), not just the abstract:

- Study 1 (UK): 1,022 recruited, 233 incomplete, **final n = 789** (242 female /
  549 male, ages 18–80, M = 41.68). Questionnaire released 3 December 2022.
- **76% agreed** that Christmas feels like it comes around more quickly each
  year, **14% neither**, **only 10% disagreed**.
- Study 2 (Iraq, Ramadan): n = 621, 70% agreed.
- The belief was associated with greater prospective-memory errors, greater
  attention to time and greater enjoyment of the event.

*Why it is used:* the "I'll order it next week" failure is a prospective-memory
failure, and this is the only dataset that measures the feeling directly, for
Christmas specifically.

**Routing note.** The canonical copies are on `journals.plos.org` (banned, at cap
3) and `pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` (banned, at cap 3). The LJMU EPrints deposit is the
same CC BY article and an untouched domain, so it is what the post links.

### S2 — Torkar (2021), *CEPS Journal* 11(4) — landing page read

`https://journals.uni-lj.si/CEPSJ/article/view/26746` · DOI `10.26529/cepsj.1110`
· published 2021-12-23.

- n = 130 University of Ljubljana pre-service teachers, surveyed January 2020.
- December 2019 giving: **65.2% at Christmas**, 54.6% Saint Nicholas Day, 10.8%
  New Year — Christmas is the most common December gift-giving occasion.
- Childhood receiving: 95.4% Saint Nicholas Day, 60.0% Christmas, 23.1% New Year.
- Sustainable behaviours named by respondents explicitly include **"giving or
  receiving nonmaterial gifts"**.

*Why it is used:* it is the only source found that names a nonmaterial gift as a
deliberate choice rather than a cheap substitute — which is exactly the claim the
post needs to make honestly.

### S3 — Batinga, Pinto & Resende (2017), *Review of Business Management* 19(66) — **abstract only**

`https://rbgn.fecap.br/RBGN/article/view/557` · DOI `10.7819/rbgn.v0i0.3429` ·
published 2017-10-06.

Discourse analysis of children's Christmas letters to Santa Claus; findings point
to discursive marks of **materialism and secularism**, with Santa Claus directly
associated with the date. **Only the abstract was read** — the full text is in
Portuguese. This is stated in the body as well as here.

*Why it is used:* it supports the post's claim that the modern Christmas gift
exchange has drifted secular, which is the bridge to the Indian secular
celebration.

### S4 — Registrar General of India, Census 2011 religion data (via PIB) — **the one generic context statistic**

`https://pib.gov.in/newsite/PrintRelease.aspx?relid=126326` · released
2015-08-25.

- Total population 2011: **121.09 crore**.
- **Christian: 2.78 crore, 2.3%.** (Hindu 79.8%, Muslim 14.2%, Sikh 1.7%.)
- Christian decadal growth 2001–2011: 15.5%, against 17.7% overall.

This is the post's **single** permitted generic context statistic.
