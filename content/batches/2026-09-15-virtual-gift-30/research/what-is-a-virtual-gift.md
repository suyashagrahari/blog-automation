## Phase 2 — Gap analysis

**Table stakes** (all five cover these, so the post must too): a plain definition of
"virtual gift"; the contrast with a physical gift; instant delivery; a step-by-step
send flow; email/SMS/link as the delivery channel.

**The gap — four findings, none of which any ranking page covers:**

1. **Every page collapses "virtual gift" into "virtual gift card".** Wikipedia's own
   `Virtual gift` entry is a *disambiguation page* listing three unrelated referents.
   No ranking page acknowledges the ambiguity; each silently picks one meaning.
2. **No usage data anywhere.** Not one of the five cites a single statistic about what
   people actually send, how long it takes, or what happens after the link is shared.
3. **Privacy is never mentioned.** No page asks whether a virtual gift can be locked to
   one recipient. On SubhSandesh 40.6% of creators password-protect before sharing.
4. **Effort is assumed to be zero.** Every page sells speed. The first-party data says
   the median build runs 6.2 hours from first save to last edit — people labour over
   these, which is the opposite of the convenience story being sold.

**Stale data:** nothing to supersede — none of the five cites any data at all, which
is itself the finding.

**Unanswered questions raised and dropped by the ranking set:** does it feel
impersonal? can you keep it private? what do you send when the point is not money?

**Subject test:** Kwon and Zhang are squarely about gifting behaviour; PayPal and
Wikipedia are about the term itself. **Peer-reviewed + open-access:** three of five.
**Generic context statistic (PIB/TRAI/Census/MEA): zero used** — deliberately, per
BRIEF §3, since "India has N internet users" is about none of these posts.

**Dropped, and why:** `https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16932` (Lee et al., messenger-based
gifting in Korea / Japan / China) was drafted in as a sixth source and then removed —
five sibling posts in this batch had already cited the same URL, breaking the two-post
URL cap and the three-post `arxiv.org` domain cap. It was **not** laundered through its
ACM mirror (`dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3757689`), which would have evaded the cap while
citing the identical study. Step 4 of the send guide was rewritten around first-party
data instead. The post therefore ships with five sources rather than six.

**Swap-test failure, recorded honestly:** Hall et al. (2022) is about communication
modality in general, so it could sit unchanged in a long-distance post elsewhere in
this batch. It is load-bearing here for one specific claim — a page is richer than a
text but poorer than a call — and it is the only one of the five that fails the test.
Searches for a study measuring rich versus lean *gift artefacts* specifically
(`digital gift richness relational closeness experiment`, `personalised digital
artefact intimacy study`) returned nothing that measures that.

**Also read but not cited:** arXiv 2108.05960 (Qiu & Klug, Douyin virtual gifting) —
the AoIR abstract carries no extractable finding; `link.springer.com/article/10.1186/
