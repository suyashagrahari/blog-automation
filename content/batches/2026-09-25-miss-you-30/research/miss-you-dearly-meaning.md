## Phase 2 — gap

**Table stakes:** a plain gloss ("miss you very much"); a note that it is warmer or more
intense than "I miss you"; a list of alternative phrasings; some statement about when it
suits.

**The gap.** Nobody treats `dearly` as the object of study. Zero of six name the word class
and cite a sense; zero name a corpus; zero separate the cost sense from the affection sense;
zero give an etymology. Two cite Merriam-Webster in passing without a sense number.

**Angle.** The only post on this SERP that makes the adverb the subject: the dictionary's
own derived-term list, the pay-dearly/miss-dearly split as two lexicalised entries, the
undefined `rfdef` sense, a corpus count, and the Old English gloss — set against 214 real
miss-you pages.

**Fan-out sub-queries → H2s.** What does *dearly* add? · Which phrases use it? · Is it the
same *dearly* as in *pay dearly*? · Where does the word come from? · Is it formal? · Is it
about death? · Which line should I actually send? · Is it right for a text?

## Prompt claims tested

| claim in the task prompt | verdict |
|---|---|
| `dearly` survives in fixed collocations: *dearly beloved, dearly departed, pay dearly, love dearly, miss dearly* | **half correct.** Wiktionary's derived terms are *sell dearly, pay dearly, sell one's life dearly, dearly departed*. No entry exists for *dearly beloved*, *love dearly* or *miss dearly*. |
| the sense in *pay dearly* differs from the sense in *miss dearly* — cost vs intensity | **confirmed and sharpened.** *pay dearly* is its own verb entry, "to suffer as a result of one's actions" — consequence, not degree. |
| *miss dearly* is the intensity sense | **did not survive.** No sense of `dearly` uses a *miss* frame as its example, and the sense used with verbs of wishing is tagged `rfdef` with no definition at all. |
| "I miss you dearly" is markedly more formal and literary; funeral-adjacent | **supported, not measured.** Dictionary labels and the Tatoeba gap support it; no register-balanced frequency comparison was run, and the post says so in body prose. |
| etymology of *dear*: OE *dēore*, precious/costly | **confirmed and extended.** Gloss is "of great value or excellence, expensive, beloved"; the Germanic cognates kept only "expensive". |
