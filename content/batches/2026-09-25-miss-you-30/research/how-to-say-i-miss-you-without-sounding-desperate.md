## Phase 3 — sources, and what each one actually says

Every claim below was pulled from the fetched record, not from memory. Where only the
abstract was readable, that is stated.

### 1. Starr, L. R., & Davila, J. (2008). *Excessive reassurance seeking, depression, and interpersonal rejection: a meta-analytic review.* Journal of Abnormal Psychology, 117(4), 762–775.
`https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013866` · published 2008-11-01 · **paywalled; abstract only**
(fetched from the PubMed record, PMID 19025224).

Verbatim from the abstract: *"A meta-analysis of 38 studies (N = 6,973) revealed an
aggregate effect size (r) of .32 between ERS and concurrent depression… A second
meta-analysis of 16 studies yielded a weighted mean effect size of .14 between ERS and
concurrent rejection… studies examining romantic relationships yielding marginally stronger
effect sizes than studies examining nonromantic relationships. Results support the ERS model
(with several important caveats)."*

**Why it matters here:** Coyne's model has two halves, and the half that matches the
reader's fear — reassurance-seeking causing rejection — is the weak one, r = .14 against
r = .32 for the depression half. Subject test: pass. Swap test: pass (fails in no other
keyword in this batch — this is the only post about perceived neediness).

**Limit recorded:** full text not read. Everything the post says about this paper is in the
abstract above.

### 2. Perks, R. E., Vowels, L. M., Hart, C. M., Francois-Walcott, R. R. R., & Carnelley, K. B. (2025). *Help! I need somebody: Development and validation of the Romantic Support-Seeking (RoSS) scale.* Current Psychology, 45(1).
`https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-025-08749-0` · published 2025-12-22 · **open access, CC BY 4.0**
· full text read via the Europe PMC OA copy (PMC12722480).

What it actually says, verified in the full text:

- *"Direct support-seeking refers to explicit requests for support, whereas indirect
  support-seeking uses behavioral cues or hints (Fraley & Shaver, 1998) – like sulking or
  whining (Don et al., 2019)."*
- Study 1 theme counts across three segments: **Ask directly 96 / 56 / 75** against
  **Hint/complain 2 / 0 / 1**.
- Study 3 (N = 355): *"Direct emotional support-seeking and direct instrumental
  support-seeking positively correlated with relationship quality, sexual satisfaction,
  perceived support, and life satisfaction… Conversely, indirect support-seeking and no
  support wanted correlated negatively with relationship quality, perceived support, and
  life satisfaction."*
- Regression: attachment anxiety positively predicted indirect support-seeking
  (B = 0.31, SE = 0.04, t = 7.90, p < .001) and **also** direct emotional support-seeking
  (B = 0.16, SE = 0.02, t = 6.81, p < .001).
- The scale's highest-loading direct-emotional item is *"I seek reassurance or comfort from
  my partner"* (0.79/0.83), and a second is *"I ask for physical comfort, such as hugs, from
  my partner"* (0.65/0.67).
- **Stated limitation, quoted in the post:** *"The samples were predominantly young, White,
  and female."*

Subject test: pass. Swap test: pass. Peer-reviewed and open-access: yes — this is the post's
scholarly open source.

### 3. Hampton, A. J., Rawlings, J., Treger, S., & Sprecher, S. (2018). *Channels of Computer-Mediated Communication and Satisfaction in Long-Distance Relationships.* Interpersona, 11(2).
`https://doi.org/10.5964/ijpr.v11i2.273` · published online 2018-02-23 · **open access**
· abstract and record read at `ojs.interpersonajournal.com`.

From the abstract: *"we found that the frequency of utilizing various channels of
communication was associated with relationship satisfaction and communication satisfaction.
The most frequently used channel of communication was text messaging. However, Skype use was
the strongest predictor of both relationship satisfaction and communication satisfaction.
Further, communication satisfaction mediated the relation between Skype use and relationship
satisfaction."*

**This is a framing correction.** The task prompt framed the Reis & Clark responsiveness
literature as showing that *"perceived responsiveness, not frequency, predicts closeness."*
In the one open long-distance study actually fetched here, **frequency was associated with
satisfaction.** What outperformed it was channel richness — video over text — with
communication satisfaction as the mediator. So the honest sentence is not "frequency doesn't
matter"; it is "frequency matters less than whether the exchange itself felt good, and a
richer channel carries that better." The post says it that way.

**Limit recorded:** abstract and journal record read; full PDF was not retrievable (the
PsychOpen mirror 404s and the journal has moved publisher). Nothing beyond the abstract is
claimed.

### 4. Floyd, K., Debrot, A., Horan, S. M., Hesse, C., & Woo, N. T. (2022). *Affectionate communication, health, and relationships.* Personal Relationships.
`https://doi.org/10.1111/pere.12444` · published 2022-09-09 · open-access copy at
`iris.unil.ch` · abstract read.

From the abstract: a systematic review of **86 individual empirical studies representing
26,013 participants**, concluding that *"U.S. Americans, women, younger individuals, white
individuals, and students are overrepresented in research samples."*

**Why it earns a place:** it is the honest ceiling on every number in this post's research
half. An Indian reader is being handed findings from a literature that barely sampled them.
Subject test: pass (affectionate communication is exactly the post's subject). Swap test:
pass.

**Limit recorded:** abstract only.

| Prompt claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Perceived responsiveness, not frequency, predicts closeness" | **Too strong.** Hampton et al. (2018) found frequency *was* associated with satisfaction in long-distance couples; video beat text, mediated by communication satisfaction. Corrected in the body. |

| Attachment anxiety → hinting | **Holds, with a caveat the prompt omits.** Anxiety predicted indirect seeking (B = 0.31) *and* direct emotional seeking (B = 0.16). "Anxious people hint, secure people ask" is too tidy; the discriminating variable is the behaviour, not the person. |
