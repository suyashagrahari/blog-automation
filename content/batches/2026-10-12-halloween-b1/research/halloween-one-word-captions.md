## Phase 2 — gap
- **Table stakes:** mood groups (funny, cute, couples, group, spooky), puns, emoji.
- **Gap:** no page gives a genuine one-word list; no page says which lines are original; no page explains what a screen reader speaks for an emoji (🧙 = "mage"); no page offers evidence on short vs long captions.
- **Inconvenient finding:** Cuevas-Molano et al. 2022 (14,067 brand posts) say longer messages are used to increase engagement — the post states one word is a style choice, not a hack.
- **Fan-out:** what makes a one-word caption work; one-word puns; one-word quotes (Poe "Nevermore"); couples/kids/group words; emoji pairing; screen-reader reading; one word vs sentence.

Angle: wins by being the only post that actually gives one-word captions, grouped by photo mood with honest original-vs-common labelling and the CLDR names screen readers speak for each paired emoji.

## Phase 3 — sources (findpapers queries: "processing fluency brevity social media engagement", "caption length Instagram engagement", "emoji screen reader accessibility blind users", "emoji interpretation ambiguity text message", "lexical blends portmanteau word formation recognition"; plus Europe PMC title search "processing fluency")
1. Okuhara et al. 2017, BMC Res Notes, europepmc PMC5465451 — full text read, CC BY 4.0. 538 records 1980–2015; fluency → liking/confidence; phonologically fluent words → favourable evaluations; plain language = common familiar words, short sentences. Caveat stated: health materials, not Instagram.
2. Cuevas-Molano, Sánchez-Cid & Gordo-Molina 2022, Communication & Society (revistas.unav.edu) — **abstract only** (security-verification wall; abstract via Crossref). 14,067 posts, 14 brands, 10 industries.
3. Miller et al. 2017, ICWSM (ojs.aaai.org) — abstract page read: 2,482 participants; context does not reduce emoji miscommunication.
4. Griggio, Gorman & Tigwell 2024, Proc. ACM HCI 8(CSCW1) (dl.acm.org, CC BY-NC-SA — cited, not reused) — **abstract only** (dl.acm.org 403; abstract via Crossref): 1,508 messages, 116 sighted participants, moving emoji to the end.
5. Unicode UTS #51 v18.0, 2026-08-28 (unicode.org, cap-exempt) — CLDR short names used for text-to-speech for accessibility. Names checked in CLDR English annotations JSON (🧙 mage, 🎃 jack-o-lantern, 🕸 spider web, 🐈‍⬛ black cat, 🔮 crystal ball, 🍬 candy, 🦇 bat, 🖤 black heart, 🌕 full moon, 🧹 broom, 🍂 fallen leaf, 💀 skull).
