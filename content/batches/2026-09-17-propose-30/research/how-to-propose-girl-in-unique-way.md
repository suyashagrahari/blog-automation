## Phase 2 — Gap analysis

**Table stakes** (all five carry these, so the post must too): the meaningful-location idea, the clue/scavenger trail, some form of scrapbook or letter, the "make it personal to her" instruction, and a distance variant.

**The gap.** Not one of the five asks whether *unique* is the thing the recipient wants, and not one distinguishes **unusual** from **memorable**. Every page treats novelty as a monotonic good: more unusual equals more unforgettable. Nobody defines memorable, nobody cites a memory finding, and nobody names the case where a spectacle is actively the wrong call for the person being proposed to.

**Stale data.** There is no data to be stale. Across the four fetchable pages, the count of cited sources, statistics and dates is zero. That is the opening — a citable claim with a date beats a 3,327-word uncited list to a retrieval system.

**Unanswered questions raised and dropped:** "pick something that reflects her personality" (how?), "make it unforgettable" (by what mechanism?), "don't overdo it" (where is the line?).

## Phase 3 — Sources

Search terms used: `self-expansion model novel activities couples study`, `novelty relationship satisfaction shared activities research`, `distinctiveness effect memory encoding`, `von Restorff isolation effect open access`, `peak-end rule experience evaluation`, `bizarreness effect mixed list design`, `autobiographical memory salience emotional events`, `perceived effort gift thoughtfulness recipient appreciation`.

Five sources. None appears in the 54 URLs spent in `content/batches/2026-09-17-propose-12`, and none is on the six-URL exclusion list handed over by the sibling agent.

1. **Geraci, McDaniel, Miller & Hughes — *The Bizarreness Effect: Evidence for the Critical Influence of Retrieval Processes*, Memory & Cognition, November 2013.** <https://openprairie.sdstate.edu/psych_pubs/9/> (institutional repository, open). Bizarre sentences beat common ones *only* when both were recalled together; recalled separately, the advantage disappeared. Retrieval of the mixed-list context, not distinctive encoding, drives the effect. → Strangeness needs an ordinary backdrop, which a wall-to-wall stunt destroys.
2. **Algoe, Gable & Maisel — *It's the little things: Everyday gratitude as a booster shot for romantic relationships*, Personal Relationships 17, 217–233, 2010.** <https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Algoe-Gable-Maisel-2010-Its-the-little-things.pdf> (author-hosted full text, read in full). Daily-experience sampling of 67 heterosexual cohabiting couples. For women, *perceived* partner thoughtfulness predicted their own gratitude significantly better than the partner's own report of having been thoughtful (χ² = 9.65, p = .002). Gratitude on one day predicted next-day increases in relationship connection and satisfaction for both partners. → Effort has to be visible to her; invisible effort does not convert.
3. **Muise lab — *Virtually Connected: Do Shared Novel Activities in Virtual Reality Enhance Self-Expansion and Relationship Quality?*, Behavioral Sciences 15(1):67, January 2025.** <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11761567/> (peer-reviewed, open access, read in full). Study 1 N = 183, Study 2 N = 141. The novel condition reliably lowered relational boredom (Study 1 M = 1.78 vs 2.80, p < .001) and at times raised closeness, but showed **no significant difference in self-expansion (p = .113) or relationship satisfaction (p = .909)**. → Novelty is a boredom treatment, not a satisfaction lever. This is the single most load-bearing finding in the post.
4. **Alaybek, Dalal, Fyffe, Aitken, Zhou, Qu, Roman & Baines — *All's well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect*, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170, 2022.** <https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:170:y:2022:i:c:s0749597822000334> — **abstract only**; the full text is behind ScienceDirect, which returns 403. 174 effect sizes; peak-end effect large (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487–0.661) but **comparable to the effect of the overall average (mean) score** across episodes, and duration essentially nil. → The peak is not better than the average. Cited only to what the abstract states, and the post says "abstract only" in its own sources list.
5. **Strijbosch, Mitas, van Gisbergen, Doicaru, Gelissen & Bastiaansen — *From Experience to Memory: On the Robustness of the Peak-and-End-Rule for Complex, Heterogeneous Experiences*, Frontiers in Psychology 10:1705, 24 July 2019.** <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01705/full> (open access, read in full). A 14-minute mixed-emotion VR film, retold immediately and again a week later. One week on, remembered valence and arousal were best predicted by **average** emotional valence and arousal, not peak or end; average arousal explained 47.2% of variance in immediate overall arousal against 21.6% for peak. → A week later, the quality of the whole evening outweighs the height of one second.

**Subject test:** sources 2 and 3 are directly about romantic couples and shared novel activity; 1, 4 and 5 are about how experiences are encoded and later evaluated, which is the post's actual claim. Requirement of ≥2 is met.
**Open-access peer-reviewed:** 3 and 5 both. Requirement of ≥1 is met.
**Swap test:** none of the five would sit unchanged in a sibling post — they are all about novelty, distinctiveness or retrospective evaluation, not about India, the internet, or messaging.
**Generic context statistics:** zero. No PIB, TRAI, Census or MEA figure appears.
**Wikipedia:** zero body links.
