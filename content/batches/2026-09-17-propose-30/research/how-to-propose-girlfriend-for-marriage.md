## Phase 2 — gap

**Table stakes** (all five): talk about marriage before you propose; match the setting to
her personality; get a ring; pick a meaningful location; rehearse a speech; have a backup
plan; hire a photographer; "get her parents' blessing".

**The gap.** Every incumbent treats the family as a *ceremonial* step — one phone call to
her father, framed as courtesy. Indian survey data says the family is a *decision-maker*,
and that the dominant form of marriage is neither "they chose" nor "I chose" but the
middle ground. Not one ranking page names the two-stage structure, the order of the ask,
or what to do in the weeks between her yes and the families' yes. Nobody covers the case
where the answer is "yes, but convince my parents", which is the modal Indian outcome.

**Stale data.** The only statistic on the whole SERP is The Knot's Real Weddings Study
(58% feel pressure to be unique), and it is US wedding-industry data used as a hook.

**Fan-out sub-queries → H2s**
1. What does a marriage proposal mean in India when two families are involved → H2 2
2. Who actually decides → H2 3
3. In what order do I ask → H2 4 (five H3 steps)
4. What format should the private ask take → H2 5 (comparison table)
5. What happens between her yes and their yes → H2 6
6. When is a digital proposal the wrong call → H2 7

**Angle.** Wins by being the only post that treats an Indian marriage proposal as a
two-stage ask — her first, then two families — with Indian survey evidence on who
actually decides, alongside SubhSandesh's platform-wide password-protection rate for the
private half.

1. **Manjistha Banerji (2008), *Is Education Associated with a Transition towards Autonomy
   in Partner Choice? A Case Study of India*, University of Maryland (DRUM).**
   <https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/8809> — **full text read** (PDF extracted).
   India Human Development Survey 2005, N = 20,854 women who first married at 15–24.
   Jointly arranged 36.52%; parent-arranged with no consent 35.36%; parent-arranged with
   consent 23.18%; fully self-arranged 4.94%. Table 4: even among self-arranged marriages,
   38.97% met the husband on the wedding day. Conclusion: spouse choice is a continuum and
   the growth category is *parent-arranged with consent*, not autonomy.
   Subject test: passes (Indian partner-choice decision-making). Swap test: passes — no
   other keyword in this batch turns on who decides a marriage.
   *Caveat recorded: an MA thesis, scholarly and open but not peer-reviewed.*

2. **Anita Raj, Hilawit Gebrehanna, Nicole E. Johns, Namratha Rao et al. (2026),
   *Measuring marital choice as an indicator of women's marital agency in rural India*,
   Contraception and Reproductive Medicine 11.** Published 26 February 2026, CC BY-NC-ND.
   <https://contraceptionmedicine.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40834-026-00437-z>
   — **full text read** via the Europe PMC REST API.
   CHARM2 trial data, N = 1,192 married women aged 18–30, rural Maharashtra. 15.5% were
   the primary decision-maker on partner selection; 9.4% on marital timing. Uncles (29.8%)
   and fathers (20.8%) were most often the primary decision-maker on partner choice;
   fathers decided timing in 66.0% of cases. 80.9% had an arranged marriage; 7.1% married
   for love without parental consent.
   **This is the peer-reviewed, open-access source the checklist requires.**

3. **Megan Reed (2025), *Roka Engagements and the Hybridization of Arranged and "Love"
   Marriage in Urban India*, Journal of Marriage and Family**, issued 9 May 2025.
   <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jomf.13115> — **ABSTRACT ONLY.** Wiley
   returned HTTP 403 to automated fetching (both the landing page and `pdfdirect`), so the
   publisher abstract was retrieved through the Crossref API and nothing beyond it is
   claimed. 48 interviews, New Delhi area, 2018–2019, college-educated middle class.
   Couples in arranged matches court during the engagement; couples in self-chosen
   relationships use the roka to signal parental endorsement and avoid stigma; that
   courtship sometimes ends in a broken engagement.

4. **"Warmi urquy": marriage proposals and gender hierarchy in the Peruvian Andes (2026),
   Frontiers in Sociology**, published 3 March 2026, DOI 10.3389/fsoc.2026.1722698.
   <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sociology/articles/10.3389/fsoc.2026.1722698/full>
   — **full text read** via the Europe PMC REST API. Ethnography in Huanta, Ayacucho: the
   marriage proposal is staged between the two families and their godparents rather than
   between the couple. Used for exactly one sentence, to show the two-family proposal form
   is not an Indian peculiarity. Peer-reviewed and open access.

5. **Shifting partnership ideals with online technologies among unmarried women in India
   (2025), Social Forces**, issued 6 July 2025, DOI 10.1093/sf/soaf100.
   <https://doi.org/10.1093/sf/soaf100> — **ABSTRACT ONLY.** academic.oup.com returned
   HTTP 403 to automated fetching; abstract read via Crossref/OpenAlex. Survey of
   unmarried, partnered women across twenty Indian states: daily internet use, and
   independent rather than shared device access, is associated with more secular views on
   partner choice and on love marriage; replicated on NFHS 2019–2021.
