The engine's summary produced "1–3 months", "5–8 dates", "at least 2 months", and closed with an explicit note that **the results contain no India-specific guidance**. |

Two findings I am willing to stand behind, because they come from what I saw rather than
from pages I did not open:

1. **The head of this SERP is answering a different question.** The first query returns
   marriage-proposal timing. The keyword asks about the girlfriend ask, which in Indian
   usage is what "propose" normally means. That mismatch is the gap.
2. **The numbers on offer contradict each other and carry no population.** Two months,
   three to six months, five to eight dates, one year, two to five years — all in one
   result set, none attached to a named sample. This is the thing the post can beat
   honestly: not by giving a better number, but by explaining why nobody has one.

**Not claimed anywhere in the post:** the position of any page, what any competitor
covers, or that "no competitor covers X". Inherited Phase 1 only; none of this batch's
keywords appears in the 11 India-served SERPs measured on 2026-09-16.

## Phase 2 — Gap and angle

**Table stakes** (present across the result titles, so the page has to touch them):
what "propose" means, how long people usually wait, signs she is interested, what to
say, what to do if the answer is no.

**The gap.** Every sibling in this territory answers *what to say*, *how to say it*, or
*where to say it*. The two named cannibalisation risks both assume the relationship
already exists:

  jentheiss.com, fincham.info, pubmed, revistas.uma.es, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. **All

  — the nerves lane, built on rejection forecasting and the liking gap (Cornell

- `content/batches/2026-09-23-propose-30b/blogs/how-to-propose-a-girl-without-knowing-her.json`
  — holds mere-exposure and Reis et al. 2011 on familiarity in live interaction
  (sas.rochester.edu). **Avoided: no mere-exposure, no Reis 2011.**

Mine sits **before all three**. The relationship does not exist, the reader is not
asking about marriage, and the question is purely *when it is reasonable to ask*.
Structure is a condition list, not a script list, and there is no "what to say" section
at all — that is deliberate, because the siblings own it.

**Angle.** Wins by being the only post that answers the timing question with conditions
and then says plainly that no number of days exists — pricing the claim against the one
measurement that reports the actual spread (1 to 180 months of friendship before
romance, N = 1,897) and against SubhSandesh's own platform-wide page mix.

**Fan-out sub-queries** → became H2s or FAQs: what does "propose" mean here · what has
to be true first · what does not count as a signal · is it too soon · is it too late ·
how long do people actually wait · what if she says no · should I send something.

## Phase 3 — Sources (4; capcheck clean at selection and again before save)

Research lane held: **relationship initiation timing and how acquaintance becomes
attraction.** Queries run through `findpapers.mjs` and Crossref/Unpaywall:
`acquaintance process attraction development`, `relationship initiation timing study`,
`reciprocal self-disclosure liking laboratory study`,
`familiarity attraction repeated interaction study`,
`transition from friendship to romantic relationship young adults`,
`romantic relationship India emerging adults dating`,
`responsiveness perceived partner intimacy process model`.

1. **Stinson, Cameron & Hoplock (2022), *Social Psychological and Personality Science*
   13(4), 562–571.** Read in full from the author-deposited CC BY-NC copy at
   `https://zenodo.org/records/5136908` (SAGE itself returns 403 to every client tried).
   Study 3: meta-analysis of seven samples, N = 1,897 Canadian and US students and
   crowdsourced adults; median proportion **.66** (95% CI [.59, .72]), **weighted mean
   68.2%** were friends before becoming romantic partners. Study 4: of 298 introductory
   psychology students, the 210 friends-first initiators had been friends a mean of
   **21.90 months** (SD 28.23; Mdn 12; Mode 12; **range 1 to 180 months**); **70.3%**
   (147 of 210) said neither person entered the friendship out of attraction; **47.4%**
   (139 of 298) named "a friendship turning romantic" the best way to meet a partner,
   against **0.3%** for an online dating service. Authors' own limitations, quoted in
   the post: retrospective reports, WEIRD samples, "friendship" left undefined.
2. **Pooja & Bhatia (2026), *Space and Culture, India* 13(4), 81–104**, CC BY, read in
   full. N = **510** unmarried urban Indian emerging adults aged 18–25 (221 men, 289
   women), university students across several Indian states. **48.4%** began their first
   romantic relationship between ages **17 and 21** — the highest-frequency band; above
   **60%** among participants from nuclear families. One of the two most common patterns
   is the **"Romantically Friendly" relationship**, defined by commitment and emotional
   closeness in which friendship with the partner and with mutual friends is central.
3. **Fingerman, Birditt, Fiori, Hall, Huxhold, Rauer, Sandstrom & Sprecher (2025),
   *Review of General Psychology*.** CC BY-NC copy read at figshare. Defines a
   **"fringeship"**: regular contact in a specific setting; volitional and **mutual**
   recognition of the connection; freedom from the obligations of close ties; shared
   affection; a balance of small talk and personal disclosure. Theoretical review, no
   new sample — used for its construct and its criteria, not for a statistic.
4. **Woo, Kwon & Yang (2014), *Korean Journal of Social and Personality Psychology*
   28(3), 111–125.** **656** high-school students in Seoul and Gyeonggi. Responsiveness
   **mediated** the self-disclosure → intimacy link offline and **moderated** it online.
   **Body text is Korean; only the English abstract was read**, so the post claims
   nothing beyond it.
