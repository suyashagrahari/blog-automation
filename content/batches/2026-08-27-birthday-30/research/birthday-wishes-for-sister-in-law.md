## Phase 2 — Gap analysis

**Table stakes** (all of them do this, so the post must too): actual usable wishes;
short/formal/warm variants; something about tone; the query phrase in the H1.

**The gap.** English *sister-in-law* is a single reference term with **four** distinct
Indian referents — *bhabhi* (brother's wife), *nanad* (husband's sister), *jethani*
(husband's elder brother's wife), *devrani* (husband's younger brother's wife). No
ranking page separates them. And two things follow from the vocabulary that no page says:

1. **Reference is not address.** All four are terms you use to *describe* the relation to
   somebody else. Almost none of them is what you *call* her. The address set is smaller,
   different and asymmetric.
2. **Rank is inherited, not earned.** *jethani* and *devrani* are morphologically derived
   from *jeth* and *dewar* — her husband's position, not her age. So you cannot read the
   register off how old she is.

**Stale data:** none of the nine cites any data at all, of any age.

**Unanswered questions raised and dropped:** what to call her; whether to use Hindi or
English; whether a wish that works for one sister-in-law works for another.

**Fan-out sub-queries → H2s:** which sister-in-law is this? · what do I actually call her? ·
is the term I use to describe her the term I use to her face? · does her age set the
register or does her husband's? · what do I write, per relation? · how long should it be? ·
what if I have no time left?

**Angle:** wins by being the only post that separates the four relations English calls
"sister-in-law", then separates the term you use *about* her from the term you use *to*
her — measured in Platts' 1884 dictionary and in the Bhojpuri vocative data — and sizes
every wish to SubhSandesh's measured 96-character median.

**Cannibalisation:** `birthday-wishes-for-bhabhi` is LIVE and owns the brother's wife.
This post covers *nanad*, *jethani* and *devrani* properly and uses *bhabhi* only as the
contrast case. `birthday-wishes-for-sister`, `birthday-wishes-for-elder-sister` are live and
`birthday-wishes-for-younger-sister` was written in wave 2 — none is restated here.

**Differentiation from `birthday-wishes-for-uncle` (written today, same shape of angle):**
that post measures *frequency* (which term appears more in a Hindi news corpus) and fixes
register per relation. This post measures *semantics* — what each term does in a dictionary
entry, whether it is a relational noun or a vocative, and where its seniority comes from
morphologically. No corpus counts, no Leipzig, no shared source, no shared H2.

### The original measurement

Run against the Digital Dictionaries of South Asia on **2026-08-27**. Two dictionaries, 109
years apart, queried the same way.

- Full-text search for the string `sister-in-law`: **Platts 20 entries, McGregor 9**. Three
  of Platts' 20 in fact gloss a male affine (*bham*, *bahnoi*, *silf*).
- **Neither dictionary gives *jethani* or *devrani* a headword gloss of "sister-in-law".**
  Platts: "The wife of a husband's elder brother" / "Husband's younger brother's wife".
  McGregor: the same, and where it does reach for English it must add a modifier —
  "junior sister-in-law", under the `-ānī` suffix entry (p. 87).
- **Address versus reference is tagged on the page in both.** Platts on *nanad* (p. 1155):
  a woman avoids "husband" "whether in addressing or speaking of her partner". McGregor on
  *nanad* (p. 543): "auspicious form of **reference** on a wife's part". Platts lists
  *bībī-jī* (p. 205) once as a noun, "husband's sister", and once as **intj.**, "(A
  respectful form of address), Lady! Madam!". McGregor's *jījī* (p. 374) is "1. an elder
  sister-in-law. 2. **voc.** form of address to a senior lady (women)."
- **The morphology carries the seniority claim.** McGregor's `-ānī` entry: the suffix "forms
  feminines for m. nouns expressing occupation or status", e.g. *kṣatrāṇī* 'wife of a
  kṣatriya', *devrānī*. So *jeth* + *-ānī* and *devar* + *-ānī* — her rank is inherited from
  her husband's birth order, not her own age. (Platts derives jeṭhānī from ज्येष्ठ + आनी;
  McGregor from `*jyeṣṭhajāni-`. Recorded honestly: the two etymologies differ.)
- **The dictionaries disagree about bhabhi.** Platts: "brother's wife". McGregor: "**elder**
  brother's wife".

### What each source actually says

**Read (2007), eScholarship copy, full PDF read.** The American Kinship Terminology is
generated from a handful of structural equations. Equation (5) — the "Sibling-in-law"
equation — is *Spouse of Child of Parent = Child of Parent of Spouse*: English equates a
sibling's spouse with a spouse's sibling by rule. Read states the logic "is not overtly
known to users of the AKT" although they apply it consistently, and that the affinal
restriction equations are "culture specific".

**Pericliev (2014), full PDF read.** The KINSHIP program's dimension list includes
"Affinity of the last connecting relative" (consanguineal / affinal) and "Seniority within
one generation" (elder / younger), and the paper names those among the features "known as
far back as Kroeber (1909) to be universally used in the languages of the world". Its
51 Serbo-Croatian kin terms admit 33,973,862,400,000,000 alternative componential models —
verified, but not used in the body.

**Gupta (2015), full PDF read — the lane-K required source.** Ten Bhojpuri films plus direct
observation; more than ten vocative interjections in three honorific levels (intimacy,
neutral, honour); honour "is also very common among affinal relationship". The finding that
matters: "in a joint family, a woman uses *ho* for the wife of her husband's younger
brother, but uses *əʤi* for the wife of her husband's elder brother", and "a wife uses
*əʤi* for both younger and elder sister of her husband and always receive *ho* from them" —
a structurally asymmetric pair. Also: "use of *re* by the wife for siblings or family of
husband regarded as highly impolite." The author records that the films under-use *əʤi*
relative to daily speech; stated in the body as a limit.

## Differentiation, stated explicitly

**Against `birthday-wishes-for-uncle` (written the same day, same shape of angle).** That
post measures **frequency** — which kin term appears more often in a Hindi news corpus — and
uses the counts to rank five uncles by how much a correct term signals. This post measures
**semantics**: what a dictionary entry does with a term, whether it is tagged as a relational
noun or as a vocative, and where its seniority comes from morphologically. No corpus counts,
no Leipzig, no shared source, no shared H2, no shared sentence. The uncle post's organising
question is *which uncle is he*; this post's is *is the word you have a word you can say to
her* — and its practical payoff is different too: fix the address form before the wording,
and read the register off her husband's birth order rather than her age.

**Against the live `birthday-wishes-for-bhabhi`.** Bhabhi is one of the four and it already
has a page. Here it appears only as the contrast case: the sibling's-spouse side of Read's
equation, the one term of the four that is genuinely vocative, and the one where the two
dictionaries disagree about scope. The three relations covered properly — nanad, jethani,
devrani — are not on that page at all. No link to it, because internal links come only from
`TEMPLATE_LINKS`.

**Against the live `birthday-wishes-for-sister` / `-elder-sister` and the wave-2
`-younger-sister`.** Nothing about a sister is restated. The post's whole premise is that
three of the nine ranking results confuse the two, and it says so.
