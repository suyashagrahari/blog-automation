## Phase 2 — Gap analysis

**Table stakes** — present on effectively all nine, so the post must carry them or look incomplete:

- What a birthday number / *mūlānk* is, and how the day of the month reduces to a single digit.
- A meaning for each of the nine numbers.
- The distinction between the birthday number and the life-path number.
- The planetary rulership that Indian practice attaches to each number.

**The gap — nobody publishes a count.** worldnumerology, numerology.com, numerologist.com and
feliciabender all publish the same nine meanings, and **not one of them publishes a frequency
distribution.** Google's own PAA asks "Which birthday number is lucky?" and "What is the rarest
birthday number?" — two questions that are, read literally, *empirical* questions about how often
each number occurs. Every ranking page answers them with tradition. None answers them with a count.

**Stale/absent data.** There is no dated statistic on any of these pages to supersede; the genre
does not cite data at all. That is the opening.

**Unanswered questions the pages raise and drop:**

- If a number is "lucky", is it more or less common? (Asked by Google; answered by nobody.)
- Why do the published nine meanings differ in detail between sources?
- Does believing a number is auspicious actually change anything measurable?

**Angle (recorded as `batchMeta.angle`):** wins by being the only post that gives the traditional
nine *mūlānk* readings and then answers Google's own "which number is lucky / which is rarest" with
a real count — 182 recorded birthday dates on SubhSandesh, χ² = 14.53 on 8 df against a 15.51
threshold, i.e. indistinguishable from chance — with the stated limits printed in the body.

**Deliberate exclusions.**

- **Lane I is off-limits.** `birthday-date-personality-test` (already written in this batch) owns the
  Barnum/Forer effect and the validity of date-based personality claims. It cites Helgertz & Scott
  (Swedish marriage register), Poškus (Barnum feedback), the pseudo-profound-bullshit paper, Edwards
  et al. on astrological belief and the NZMJ knee-arthroplasty zodiac paper. **None of that
  literature is touched here, and no source is shared with it.** This post is about what the practice
  *is*, who acts on it, and what the numbers do — not whether a personality description is valid.
- No ranking of the nine numbers, no "7 is the most common Indian birth number", and no per-number
  frequency table. n=182 supports the aggregate uniformity result and nothing about any one number.
- Neither an endorsement nor a debunk. Belief is reported as belief, the measurement as a
  measurement, side by side.

## Phase 3 — Sources (all fetched and verified)

Five sources. In-batch domain counts measured from the repository root across
`content/batches/2026-08-26-birthday/blogs/*.json` (25 files) before adding any of them: all five
domains sat at **0 of 3**.

### 1. Kaur, Shukla, Mathur & Mathur (2019) — *Odd hours auspicious caesarean sections*

- `https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/4249/2891`
- Int J Community Med Public Health 2019;6(3):1358–63. `citation_date` on the article page: **2019-02-22**.
- Open access, CC BY-NC. **Full text read** (6 pp., extracted locally).
- Verified: in a South Indian teaching hospital, of 1,762 caesarean deliveries 44 (2.5%) were on
  maternal request, and **20% of those requests cited astrological concerns**; **69% of
  obstetricians have been found to accede** to patient requests for elective caesarean section.
- Lane H, India-specific: the practice of choosing an auspicious date/time, and its cost.

### 2. Antipov & Pokryshevskaya (2015) — *Are buyers of apartments superstitious?*

- `https://journal.sjdm.org/15/151009/jdm151009.pdf`
- Judgment and Decision Making 10(6):590–592, **November 2015**. CC BY. **Full text read.**
- Verified: 201 observations, Saint Petersburg primary market, collected November 2014 (34 buildings
  for floors 6–8, 33 for floors 12–14, identical floor plans, floor-independent prices). Share of
  apartments sold: **floor 13 = 54.9%** against floor 12 = 64.8% and floor 14 = 61.8%
  (t = 4.884, p < 0.001); **floor 7 = 68.6%**, the highest of the six (t = 1.962, p = 0.029).
- Why it earns its place: it isolates the number from everything else, so it measures belief rather
  than the number.

### 3. Antipov & Pokryshevskaya (2020) — *Impact of superstitious beliefs on the timing of marriage and childbirth: Evidence from Denmark*

- `https://journal.sjdm.org/20/200510/jdm200510.pdf`
- Judgment and Decision Making 15(5):756–782, **September 2020**. CC BY. **Full text read.**
- Verified: daily Danish marriage and birth data, **2007–2019**. Other things equal, **25% fewer
  marriages** occur on a non-Friday 13th than on the 14th, and **39% fewer on Friday the 13th**;
  but only **2% fewer children are born on the 13th** than on the adjacent days (p < 0.001).
- This is the analytical hinge of the post: belief moves a *chosen* date by a quarter to two-fifths
  and a *delivered* date by 2%.
- **Disclosed overlap:** same authors and same domain as source 2. Two URLs on one domain inside one
  post; `journal.sjdm.org` therefore counts as 1 of 3 posts in this batch, so the cap holds. The
  papers use different data, different countries and answer different questions.

### 4. Almond, Chee, Sviatschi & Zhong (2015) — *Auspicious birth dates among Chinese in California*

- `https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26160600/`
- Economics & Human Biology 2015 Jul;18:153–9.
- **ABSTRACT ONLY.** The full text is paywalled at Elsevier; the only Unpaywall location is the
  publisher's accepted manuscript, which returned 403. Recorded as abstract-only in the audit.
- Verified from the abstract: **2.3% "too many" Chinese-American births** in California on the
  8th, 18th or 28th, with no corresponding increase among Whites; **repeat C-sections 6% "too
  high"** on those dates; the increase is driven entirely by sons; and the 4th, 14th and 24th are
  avoided for repeat C-sections.

### 5. Cui, Gavriilidis, Gebka & Kallinterakis (2024) — *Numerological superstitions and market-wide herding: Evidence from China*

- `https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/119010/8/1-s2.0-S1057521924001315-main.pdf`
- International Review of Financial Analysis 93:103199, published **2024-03-12**. CC BY, published
  version, University of Reading CentAUR institutional repository (preferred over the Elsevier page
  per the batch rule). **Full text read.**
- Verified: Shanghai and Shenzhen exchanges, **2000–2020**; stocks classed lucky (ticker contains
  6, 8 or 9 and no 4) or unlucky (contains 4, no lucky digit). Finding: **no compelling evidence**
  that herding is stronger in superstitious stocks than in the rest of the market.

## Phase 0 — first-party facts used

From BRIEF-WAVE3.md §1 (probe of `birthdaygfpages`, 2026-08-27, 209 pages, 182 with a recorded date)
and BRIEF.md §1 (probe of 2026-08-26, n=198):

1. *mūlānk* distribution across 182 recorded birthday dates: χ² = 14.53 on 8 df against a critical
   15.51 at p = .05 — **not** significantly different from chance. Confirmed on two independent cuts
   (n=56 with a plausible year of birth, χ² = 14.07; n=126 upcoming-occurrence rows, χ² = 5.24).
2. Days 1–15 hold **28.0%** of the 182 dates where 48.4% would be expected, and the skew persists in
   the clean year-of-birth subset at **32.1% (n=56)**. Not understood.
3. Only **56 of 182** rows carry a plausible year of birth (1990–2012); **32 of 182 (17.6%)** record
   a birthday identical to the page's creation date.
4. Median lead time **0 days**; **91 of 182 (50.0%)** pages are created on the birthday itself;
   **23 of 182 (12.6%)** after it had passed.
5. Median personal message **96 characters** (n=197).
6. A quiz is built on **195 of 198** pages (98.5%) and played on only **28** of those (14.4%).

All six appear in the body; items 1, 4 and 5 land inside the first 150 words or immediately after.
