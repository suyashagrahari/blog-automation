## Phase 2 — Gap and angle

The keyword is a **definition query**. The searcher wants to know what "Propose Day"
means. The gap is that the genuinely useful answer is semantic rather than
calendrical: in Hindi and Indian English, **propose karnā does not mean "ask to
marry"** — it means to confess romantic interest and ask someone to be your
girlfriend or boyfriend. That single shift explains why Propose Day is not a mass
engagement day, why "I proposed her" is idiomatic in India, and why the entire
Valentine-week framing works.

**Angle:** the post is about the *word*, and it is the only page in this cluster that
traces the shift to a documented contact mechanism (the Hindi "X karnā" light-verb
construction) and then says plainly which of the two senses each SubhSandesh template
serves.

## Phase 3 — Sources

Research lane assigned: *semantic shift in borrowed verbs — English loanwords in
Hindi and Indian English*. All discovery went through `findpapers.mjs`
(Crossref + Europe PMC), the DOAJ API, the CORE API and two WebSearch calls.

### What was used (4 sources; every one fetched and read)

1. **Wichmann & Wohlgemuth, "Loan verbs in a typological perspective"** (2005,
   MPI-EVA / Leiden; author-hosted green OA PDF at linguist.de). **Full text read.**
   Verified in the PDF: four major strategies for borrowing a verb — the light verb
   strategy, indirect insertion, direct insertion, paradigm transfer; a sample of
   "over 60 languages in 72 donor-recipient combinations"; and, in the list of
   languages using a phrasal 'do'-verb to accommodate loan verbs,
   *"– Hindi [Indo-Aryan] < English (Kachru 1978)"*. This is the direct citable
   evidence that Hindi integrates English verbs via a 'do' light verb — i.e. karnā.
   Subject test: PASS. Swap test: PASS (no other post in this batch is about verb
   borrowing).

2. **Vaidya, Rambow & Palmer, "Syntactic composition and selectional preferences in
   Hindi Light Verb Constructions"**, *Linguistic Issues in Language Technology* 17
   (2019), University of Colorado Boulder, CC BY 4.0. **Peer-reviewed, open access,
   full text read.** Verified in the PDF: light verbs in Hindi "can combine with
   another verb, an adjective, adverb or even a borrowed English verb or noun", and
   such complex predication is "highly productive" in Hindi and other South Asian
   languages; the example LVC *chorii kar* 'theft do; steal'; and the agreement rule
   that where no nominative argument is available "the verb shows 'default'
   agreement with third person, masculine, singular" — which is what licenses
   मैंने उसे प्रपोज़ किया. Subject test: PASS.

3. **Sharifi Moghaddam, "A Semantic Change Model for French Loanwords in Persian"**,
   *Acta Linguistica Asiatica* 3(2), 2013 (University of Ljubljana, DOAJ, open
   access). **Abstract read; full PDF not fetched — disclosed in the audit.**
   Verified in the abstract: 1,878 French loanwords extracted from Zomorrodian
   (1994); 330 found to have undergone a type of semantic change. Used only for that
   figure and for the general point that borrowing a form is not borrowing a meaning.
   Subject test: PASS (loanword semantic change is the mechanism of this post).

4. **Singha, "Loanword Adaptation Across Time: Evidence from Midnapuri Dialect of
   Bangla"**, eSocialSciences (eSSH) repository. **Full text read.** Verified in the
   PDF: English *massacre* → /mætʃaka:r/ "confusion, haphazard"; *assault* →
   /aʃɔlto:/ "insult, demoralize"; *military* in "military hotel" → "non-vegetarian".
   An Indo-Aryan, Indian instance of exactly the phenomenon. **Not a peer-reviewed
   journal article** — a working-paper repository — and it is not counted toward the
   peer-review requirement; sources 2 and 3 carry that.

## Phase 4–5 — Hindi ledger and verification

**Verification method.** Morphology was checked against fetched sources, not
intuition:

- The **light-verb mechanism** (English verb + करना) — Wichmann & Wohlgemuth
  (Hindi < English listed under the light verb strategy), the 2019 *LiLT* paper
  ("even a borrowed English verb or noun", "highly productive"), and English
  Wiktionary's करना usage note, all fetched and read.
- **Default perfective agreement** in मैंने उसे प्रपोज़ किया (ergative subject,
  case-marked object → 3rd person masculine singular) — the *LiLT* PDF, which states
  that where no nominative argument is available "the verb shows 'default' agreement
  with third person, masculine, singular".
- **The register system** तू (intimate / non-honorific), तुम (familiar), आप
  (respectful) — Wikipedia's *Hindustani verbs* article, fetched, which sets out the
  four conjugation sets: mãĩ, tū, tum, āp.
